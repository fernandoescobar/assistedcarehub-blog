For a small assisted living home in Coeur d'Alene, Post Falls or Sandpoint, winter brings a familiar set of problems: snow on the walkways, ice at the entrance, a delayed delivery, and the occasional loss of power. None of these is unusual. What matters is whether your team knows, on the day, who does what.

This article walks through the emergency preparedness requirements in Idaho's assisted living rule, then offers a practical winter checklist. It is general guidance for owners and administrators, not legal or clinical advice. Your licensing agency, your nurse and your pharmacist are the right people to confirm details for your own home.

## 1. Start with what Idaho requires

Idaho's rule for residential assisted living facilities is IDAPA 16.03.22, and it applies the same way across the state. Section 155 says each facility must develop and implement an emergency preparedness plan "in the event of fire, explosion, natural disaster, or other emergency." A severe winter storm fits comfortably under natural disaster or other emergency.

Section 155 has two specific parts:

- **Relocation agreements.** The facility must have a current written agreement with two separate locations to which residents would be relocated if the building is evacuated and cannot be reoccupied.
- **Written procedures.** These must set out each person's responsibilities, where and how residents are to be evacuated, and how emergency agencies are notified.

If your plan was written for fire and has never been read with winter in mind, now is a good time to read it again.

## 2. Test the plan against a winter scenario

A plan that exists on paper is not the same as a plan that staff can follow at 3 AM. Pick one realistic scenario and talk it through with every shift, for example: the power fails during a snowstorm and the roads are slow.

Ask these questions out loud:

1. Who is in charge on each shift, and who do they call?
2. Where are the emergency telephone numbers? Section 405.03 requires a telephone on the premises for staff use in an emergency, with emergency numbers posted near it.
3. If the building had to be evacuated, which of your two relocation sites would you use, and how would residents get there on snowy roads?
4. Do the people named in your agreements still have the same contact details?

Write down anything staff were unsure about, and fix the plan, not the people.

## 3. Check your relocation agreements before the snow

Written agreements age quickly. Contact names change, a site changes ownership, or a location that was easy to reach in July is harder in January. Section 155.01 asks for a current agreement, so a yearly check is sensible.

Confirm that each of the two sites is still willing, that the agreement is signed and dated, and that the two sites are genuinely separate. Keep a copy where the shift lead can find it quickly, and include the route and the contact number in your written procedures.

## 4. Winter checks for a Coeur d'Alene assisted living home

Section 250.07 of the rule sets out heating requirements. The heating system must be capable of holding the required indoor temperature range, which the rule gives as a minimum of 70°F and a maximum of 78°F during the day, and a minimum of 62°F and a maximum of 75°F at night. Wood stoves, gas fireplaces and solid-burning fireplaces are not permitted as the sole source of heat. Portable heating devices of any kind are prohibited in resident sleeping areas.

That last point matters in an outage, when a space heater can look like a quick answer. It is not an allowed one in sleeping areas. Decide in advance what your alternative is and who you would call.

A short winter checklist:

- Have the heating system serviced before the cold sets in, and keep the service record.
- Keep walkways, ramps and the main entrance clear, with a named person and a supply of ice melt and shovels.
- Keep flashlights and spare batteries where every shift knows to look.
- Keep a supply of drinking water and nonperishable food. Section 455 already requires a seven-day supply of nonperishable foods and a two-day supply of perishable foods, so confirm your stock matches your menus.
- Keep a charged phone, power bank and a battery-backed way to reach staff.
- Know who services your furnace and who clears your road or driveway, and keep both numbers in the plan.

## 5. Plan for a power outage

The federal Ready.gov guidance on power outages is a useful reference for the practical details. It notes that an outage can disrupt communications and water, prevent the use of medical devices, and cause food spoilage. Several of its tips apply directly to a care home:

- Keep refrigerators and freezers closed. A refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about 48 hours.
- Discard food that has been above 40 degrees for two hours or more.
- If you use a generator, run it only outdoors, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages, and install carbon monoxide detectors.
- Never use a gas stove or oven to heat the building.
- Have alternate plans for refrigerating medicines and for any power-dependent medical devices, and talk to the nurse and pharmacist about them well ahead of time.

Medications deserve a conversation before an outage, not during one. Ask your pharmacist how long each refrigerated medication may be kept if the power is off, and write the answer into your plan. The decisions belong to the nurse and pharmacist; the home's job is to have the numbers and the process ready.

## 6. Keep people warm and safe

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that winter storms can bring cold temperatures, power failures, loss of communication services and icy roads, and that hypothermia and frostbite are risks of exposure to extreme cold. In a care home, that means paying extra attention to residents' rooms, blankets and clothing, and to anyone who goes outside. Ask your nurse what signs staff should watch for and when to call for help.

Also consider staffing. If snow makes it hard for the next shift to arrive, who stays, who is on call, and how is that decided? Section 600 requires staff to be up and awake and immediately available during resident sleeping hours, so a plan for covering a late relief worker belongs in your winter preparations.

## 7. Write it down and review it

After your walk-through, update the plan, date the changes and tell every shift. Keep a short record of when you reviewed it and who took part. If a drill or a real event reveals a gap, note what you changed.
