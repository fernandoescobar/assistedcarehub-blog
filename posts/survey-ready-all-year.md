Every licensed assisted living home is inspected by its state. The inspection is usually called a survey, and for most owners and administrators it is a source of worry. The worry is rarely about the care itself. It is about whether the paperwork, schedules and logs can be found and are up to date on the day the surveyor arrives.

This article explains in general terms what surveys look at, uses Idaho as a worked example, and suggests a simple yearly rhythm for keeping records ready. Requirements differ by state. Your state's own licensing agency is the authority on what applies to your home, and its rules may differ from the examples below.

## 1. What a survey is

A survey is a review by a person authorized by the licensing agency to determine whether a home is meeting the state's statutes and rules. In Idaho, the rule defines a surveyor as someone authorized by the Department of Health and Welfare to conduct surveys or complaint investigations (IDAPA 16.03.22, section 010).

Most states use the same few kinds of visit, though the names and timing vary:

- **Initial and licensure surveys**, which check a home when it first opens and then at regular intervals.
- **Follow-up surveys**, which check whether problems found earlier were corrected.
- **Complaint investigations**, which look into a specific allegation and are often unannounced.

## 2. How Idaho handles it, as an example

Idaho's rules and statute give a clear picture of how the process can work:

- Under Idaho Code section 39-3355A, initial, licensure and follow-up inspections are scheduled in advance, with the facility receiving at least 30 days' notice. Complaint investigations are made without prior notice.
- Inspectors may examine the quality of care and service delivery, the facility's records and resident accounts, and the physical premises. They may interview the licensee, administrator, staff and residents. Resident interviews are confidential and private unless the resident specifies otherwise.
- Under IDAPA 16.03.22, section 127, a home with no core issue deficiencies on two consecutive licensure surveys is inspected at least every 36 months. A home with core issue deficiencies is surveyed at least every 15 months.
- Non-core issues must be corrected within 30 calendar days of the exit conference. If the home cannot meet that timeframe, it must tell the Department within the same 30 days (section 127.03).
- Survey findings for Idaho facilities are posted on a public portal, as noted in the rule's introduction, so families and the public can see them.

If your home is outside Idaho, look up the equivalent statute and rule on your state agency's website. Do not assume the timing or terms above apply to you.

## 3. The records surveyors commonly ask to see

The exact list depends on the state, but the same categories come up again and again. Using Idaho's rule as a guide, these include:

- **The license and annual renewal.** The current license must be posted and clearly visible (section 100). The annual report and renewal application are due at least 30 days before the license expires (section 115).
- **Background checks.** Employees and contractors with direct resident access need a criminal history and background check (section 009).
- **Nursing assessments.** The facility nurse must visit at least once every 90 days to conduct quarterly assessments, and assessments must be documented, dated and signed (section 305).
- **Medication storage and records.** Medications must be kept in a locked area, and items needing cold storage must have their temperature monitored and documented weekly (section 310).
- **Staffing and training.** Staff must be up and awake during resident sleeping hours, and at least one direct care staff member certified in first aid and CPR must be in the facility at all times (section 600). Training records and certifications belong in personnel files.
- **Facility records.** Section 330 sets requirements for facility records, including a three-year minimum retention period and a requirement that paper records be legible and in ink.
- **Written policies.** Many policies are required, and the surveyor may ask how staff were told about them.

Check the current text of your own state's rule before building your list. Rules are revised, and Idaho's chapter was updated with a July 1, 2026 effective date for many sections.

## 4. Keep records ready all year: a simple rhythm

Gathering documents the week before a survey is stressful and error-prone. A steady rhythm works better, and none of it requires special tools.

**Daily**

- Care notes are entered every shift, signed, and dated.
- Anything that needs the nurse's or administrator's attention is flagged the same day.

**Weekly**

- Refrigerator temperatures for medications are logged and reviewed.
- The staff schedule is compared with what was actually worked, and coverage for overnight and CPR certification is confirmed.

**Monthly**

- Review upcoming expiration dates: staff certifications, training hours, and any other dated item.
- Confirm that the next nurse visit is on the calendar well inside the 90-day window.

**Quarterly and yearly**

- Check that each resident's nursing assessment is current.
- Put the license renewal and annual report on the calendar at least 60 days ahead, so the 30-day deadline is never close.
- Review written policies, date any changes, and record that staff were told.

## 5. A short checklist before a survey is announced

- Can you find each resident's current records within a few minutes?
- Is every staff file complete, with background check, training and certification records?
- Are medication logs, counts and storage in order, and are gaps explained in writing?
- Is the license posted and the renewal on schedule?
- Do staff know who speaks with the surveyor, and that they should answer honestly and ask for help if unsure?
- Are all previous survey findings corrected and documented?

## 6. If a survey finds problems

Findings are a normal part of the process, and what matters is the response. Read the written report carefully, note each deadline, and assign one person to each item. In Idaho, for example, the 30-day correction window for non-core issues starts at the exit conference, and core issues involve a written report and a plan of correction under Idaho Code section 39-3355A. If something is unclear, ask your licensing agency, and consider asking your attorney or a consultant familiar with your state's rules before responding to serious findings.
